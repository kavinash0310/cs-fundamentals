# Chapter 1 — English Grammar and Usage

## 1. Parts of speech and sentence structure

A sentence usually has a subject and a predicate. The subject names who or what the sentence is about; the predicate says what the subject does or is. A noun names; a pronoun replaces a noun; a verb expresses action or state; an adjective describes a noun; an adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb; a preposition relates a noun phrase; a conjunction joins words or clauses.

In “The careful teacher explained the diagram clearly,” teacher is the noun, careful is an adjective, explained is the verb, and clearly is an adverb. A phrase can come between the subject and verb without becoming the subject: “The set of answers is ready.” The head noun set is singular.

## 2. Tense and agreement

| Meaning | Example |
|---|---|
| Habit or fact, present simple | She teaches computer science. |
| Action in progress, present continuous | She is teaching a lesson. |
| Completed past event | She taught yesterday. |
| Past action connected to now | She has taught this topic before. |
| Earlier of two past events | She had prepared the notes before class began. |

A finished time such as yesterday normally takes past simple: “I submitted the form yesterday,” not “I have submitted the form yesterday.”

Agreement examples:
- Each of the students has a notebook.
- The list of chapters is long.
- The chapters in the list are detailed.
- Neither plan is ready.

With either…or and neither…nor, exam conventions commonly make the verb agree with the nearer subject. Rewrite awkward sentences when ordinary prose allows.

## 3. Articles, prepositions, and conjunctions

Use a/an for a nonspecific singular count noun, selected by sound: an hour, a university. Use the for a particular or already identified noun. General plural or uncountable nouns often take no article: Computers process data; Water is essential.

Learn prepositions in phrases: depend on, interested in, responsible for, different from. Conjunctions signal logic: because gives a reason; although introduces contrast; therefore signals a result; unless gives a condition. Do not duplicate contrast markers: use “Although it was late, we continued,” not “Although it was late, but we continued.”

## 4. Voice, reported speech, and common corrections

Active voice emphasizes the doer: “The teacher checked the answer.” Passive voice emphasizes the action or receiver: “The answer was checked by the teacher.” Passive form uses a form of be plus past participle; keep tense and number correct.

Direct: He said, “I am ready.” Reported: He said that he was ready. Pronouns and time references may shift depending on context; do not change them mechanically if the reporting situation is unchanged.

Common errors:
- “She writes neat.” → “She writes neatly.” The verb needs an adverb.
- “One of the books are missing.” → “One of the books is missing.” The subject is one.
- “More better” → “better.” Do not mark the comparative twice.
- “Discuss about the topic” → “Discuss the topic.” Discuss takes a direct object.

## 5. Comprehension and vocabulary

Read the question, identify the passage's claim and evidence, then eliminate options that add facts. Inference means what is supported, not merely plausible. For a word in context, use nearby examples, contrast, and cause-and-effect clues. An idiom is not necessarily literal: “break the ice” means reduce social tension.

### Practice with answers

1. “Each of the teachers ___ a plan.” Answer: has; each is singular.
2. “An useful lesson” or “a useful lesson”? Answer: a useful lesson; useful begins with a consonant /y/ sound.
3. Correct: “Although it was raining, but they played.” Answer: “Although it was raining, they played.”
4. Choose the inference: “The school opened a computer lab, but the report gives no data about student use.” Answer: A lab opened; student use is unknown.
5. Replace “the experiment was brief” with the closest meaning. Answer: short in duration.
6. Correct: “The results depends on the method.” Answer: “The results depend on the method.”
7. Choose passive: “The committee approved the plan.” Answer: “The plan was approved by the committee.”
8. Why should a comprehension answer stay close to the passage? Outside assumptions can make a plausible answer unsupported.
