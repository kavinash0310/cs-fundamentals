# Chapter 7 — Environment, Sustainability, and Disasters

## 1. Ecosystems and biodiversity

An ecosystem includes organisms, their physical environment, and interactions among them. Producers capture energy, consumers transfer it, and decomposers recycle nutrients. Food chains show one path; food webs show interacting paths. Energy decreases at higher trophic levels because organisms use energy for life processes.

Biodiversity includes variation within species, between species, and among ecosystems. Habitat loss, invasive species, pollution, overharvesting, and climate change can reduce biodiversity. Conservation may protect habitats in place (in situ) or preserve species outside habitats (ex situ).

## 2. Pollution and climate

Air pollution includes particulate matter and harmful gases from transport, industry, burning, and other sources. Water pollution can come from sewage, industrial discharge, agricultural runoff, and plastics. Waste management prioritizes reduction, reuse, recycling, safe treatment, and responsible disposal.

Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation. Human activities have increased their concentration, strengthening warming. Mitigation reduces emissions or increases removals; adaptation reduces harm from impacts already occurring. They are complementary.

## 3. Bihar hazards and preparedness

Flood risk depends on hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. Heavy rainfall, river flow, sediment, drainage, embankments, land use, warning access, and housing all shape outcomes. A flood is not just a natural event: planning and unequal vulnerability influence the disaster.

Heat waves require local alerts, drinking water, shaded rest, adjusted work/school hours, and care for vulnerable people. Drought response includes water conservation, crop planning, groundwater management, and livelihood support. Preparedness includes early warning, evacuation routes, accessible shelters, first aid, and reliable communication.

~~~text
risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability
reduce risk by: warning + safer location + resilient services + preparedness
~~~

This is a planning model, not a precise numerical law.

3. Why can two districts facing similar rain have different disaster losses? Exposure, housing, drainage, warning, services, and vulnerability differ.

## 5. Systems, cycles, and disaster management

### Energy and matter in an ecosystem

Energy enters most ecosystems through sunlight captured by producers. It passes through consumers and decomposers, with energy lost as heat at each transfer. Matter such as carbon, nitrogen, and water cycles between organisms and the physical environment. This is why matter can be recycled while energy flows through the system.

~~~text
sun -> plants / producers -> herbivores -> predators
                |                |
                +---- dead matter+
                         |
                   decomposers -> nutrients in soil
~~~

A food web is more realistic than a single food chain because organisms often eat or are eaten by multiple species. Removing one species can affect several links.

### Pollution and resource decisions

- Air: reduce sources, improve public/active transport, control emissions, and monitor exposure. A citywide average may hide neighborhoods near a source.
- Water: prevent untreated sewage and industrial discharge, protect watersheds, and test drinking-water quality. Clear appearance does not prove safety.
- Soil: excessive chemical input, erosion, salinity, and waste can reduce soil functions.
- Solid waste: first avoid unnecessary material, then reuse, recycle, treat, and dispose safely. Recycling is not a substitute for waste prevention.
- Biodiversity: protect habitats and ecological connections; community use and conservation planning should consider local livelihoods.

### Climate response

Mitigation addresses causes by lowering greenhouse-gas emissions or increasing removals. Adaptation addresses exposure and vulnerability through resilient buildings, warnings, heat plans, water management, and livelihood support. One project can support both, but explain which mechanism applies.

### Disaster cycle and Bihar use cases

~~~text
prevention / mitigation -> preparedness -> warning
             ^                             |
             |                             v
      recovery / safer rebuild <- response / relief
~~~

For flood planning, map hazard and exposure, keep warnings understandable, identify accessible shelters and routes, protect drinking water, and plan for people with disabilities, older adults, children, and livestock. After a flood, safe water and sanitation reduce secondary health risks.

For extreme heat, monitor official alerts, ensure drinking water and shade, adjust strenuous outdoor activity, and check on people at higher risk. For drought, combine water conservation, crop and livelihood planning, groundwater monitoring, and support for affected households. Local disaster plans and official advisories should control operational action.

The expression risk ≈ hazard × exposure × vulnerability is a **conceptual model**: reducing any factor can reduce expected harm, but it is not a literal universal calculation.
