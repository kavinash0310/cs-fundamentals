# Chapter 2 — Quantitative Aptitude and Data Interpretation

## 1. Ratios, percentages, and rates
Ratio a:b compares quantities; scale both by the same nonzero factor. Percentage change = (new-old)/old×100%. Successive changes multiply: +10% then -10% gives 1.1×0.9=0.99, a 1% decrease.

Speed=distance/time; work rate adds for independent workers. Keep units consistent.

## 2. Powers, logarithms, and estimation
Rules: aᵐaⁿ=aᵐ⁺ⁿ; log(ab)=log a+log b; log(aᵏ)=k log a. Estimate by rounding only after preserving the needed precision and order of magnitude. Check whether a result is plausible.

## 3. Counting and series
Use permutations when order matters, combinations when it does not. Arithmetic sequence adds common difference; geometric sequence multiplies common ratio. Sum formulas:
~~~text
1+...+n = n(n+1)/2
geometric: a+ar+...+ar^(n-1)=a(r^n-1)/(r-1), r≠1
~~~

## 4. Geometry, statistics, probability
Area rectangle=lw, triangle=bh/2, circle=πr²; volume prism=base area×height, cylinder=πr²h. Mean=sum/count; median is middle after sorting. Probability of equally likely event= favorable/total. Use units and diagrams.

## 5. Data interpretation
Read graph title, axes, units, legend, and scale before calculating. For pie chart, sector percentage=angle/360×100. Distinguish absolute change from percentage change and verify table totals.

Example: sales rise from 80 to 100: increase 20, percentage rise 25%, not 20%.

## 6. Questions and practice
**Q1.** 20% of 150? **A:** 30.
**Q2.** 3 workers finish in 8 days at equal rates. How long for 6 workers? **A:** 4 days, assuming same efficiency.
**Q3.** Median of 2,3,3,10,12? **A:** 3.
**Q4.** Probability of sum 7 on two fair dice? **A:** 6/36=1/6.

## Reading-comprehension worked method

Passage claim: “Urban trees reduce heat, but benefits vary by species and placement.” A supported inference is that planting location matters. “All cities should plant only one species” is unsupported and contradicts variation.

For sentence correction, first find subject and main verb, then check agreement and tense. Ignore intervening phrases: “The quality of the examples is/are important”—head noun quality is singular, so is.

For sequencing, link pronouns to antecedents and transitions:
1. introduce the object/person,
2. describe an action,
3. use “this result” to refer to that action,
4. conclude with consequence.
Chronology alone may not determine order.

**Practice:** Summarize a 4-sentence paragraph; identify one inference and one fact; edit “Each of the students have submitted their work” according to formal test convention (has).

## Extended lesson: percentage, work, and data interpretation

**Successive percentage change.** A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the start. Starting at 100: 100×1.20=120; 120×0.80=96, so net change is -4%. For a value V, apply each change multiplicatively: V(1+r1)(1+r2).

**Work rates.** If A completes a task in 6 days, rate=1/6 job/day. If B takes 3 days, combined rate=1/6+1/3=1/2 job/day, so together they finish in 2 days. Do not average completion times; add rates. If B joins after A works alone for 2 days, A has completed 2/6=1/3, leaving 2/3. At combined rate 1/2, the remaining work takes (2/3)/(1/2)=4/3 days.

### Ratios and averages

If a mixture contains milk:water=3:2 and total volume is 25 L, one ratio-part is 25/5=5 L, so milk=15 L and water=10 L. Averages are weighted by counts: if 10 scores average 80 and 5 scores average 92, combined mean=(10×80+5×92)/15=84.

### Read the units in a chart

Suppose a table reports sales: Q1=120 units, Q2=150, Q3=135. Q1→Q2 growth=(150-120)/120=25%. Q2→Q3 change=(135-150)/150=-10%. The base belongs in the denominator. If the chart is in thousands, multiply only when the question asks for absolute units; percentage change is unchanged.

### Timed solving routine

- Estimate magnitude before exact arithmetic to catch misplaced decimals.
- Convert units once and carry them consistently.
- For rates, use distance = speed × time and work = rate × time.
- For ratios, sum parts before assigning amounts.
- Skip a long calculation when an easier option is already eliminated; return if time remains.

**Check:** A price falls 10% from 500, then rises 10%: 500×0.9×1.1=495, a net 1% fall. A cyclist rides 60 km in 2 hours: average speed=30 km/h.

## 7. Exponents and logarithms: solve, do not guess

For positive a, exponent laws include aᵐaⁿ=aᵐ⁺ⁿ, aᵐ/aⁿ=aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a≠0), (aᵐ)ⁿ=aᵐⁿ, and a⁰=1 (a≠0). A negative exponent means reciprocal: a⁻ⁿ=1/aⁿ. Fractional exponents represent roots: a^(1/2)=√a for a≥0.

A logarithm answers “to what power?” log_b(x)=y means bʸ=x, with b>0, b≠1, and x>0. Thus log₂(8)=3 because 2³=8. Product and power rules are log_b(xy)=log_b(x)+log_b(y) and log_b(xᵏ)=k log_b(x).

**Worked examples**

- 2³×2⁴=2⁷=128.
- 3⁻²=1/9.
- log₁₀(1000)=3; log₂(32)=5.
- If 2ˣ=64, then x=6. If log₃(x)=4, then x=81.
- Estimate √50 between 7 and 8 because 7²=49 and 8²=64; √50 is about 7.07.

A common trap is log(x+y)≠log(x)+log(y). The product rule applies to multiplication, not addition.

## 8. Counting, sequences, and series

For n distinct objects, arranging all n gives n!. An ordered selection of r from n is P(n,r)=n!/(n-r)!. An unordered selection is C(n,r)=n!/[r!(n-r)!]. Example: choosing a chair and vice-chair from 5 people gives 5×4=20 assignments; choosing a two-person committee gives C(5,2)=10.

An arithmetic sequence has constant difference d: aₙ=a₁+(n−1)d. Its first n terms sum to n(a₁+aₙ)/2. A geometric sequence has constant ratio r: aₙ=a₁rⁿ⁻¹; for r≠1 its first n terms sum to a₁(rⁿ−1)/(r−1).

Worked check: 4, 7, 10, 13 has d=3; its 20th term is 4+19×3=61. The sum of the first 20 terms is 20(4+61)/2=650.

## 9. Geometry and mensuration

Draw and label the shape before choosing a formula. Use squared units for area and cubed units for volume.

| Shape | Area or volume |
|---|---|
| Rectangle, sides l and w | Area = lw; perimeter = 2(l+w) |
| Triangle, base b and perpendicular height h | Area = bh/2 |
| Parallelogram | Area = base × perpendicular height |
| Trapezoid, parallel sides a and b | Area = (a+b)h/2 |
| Circle, radius r | Circumference = 2πr; area = πr² |
| Rectangular prism | Volume = lwh |
| Cylinder | Volume = πr²h; curved surface area = 2πrh |
| Sphere | Volume = 4πr³/3; surface area = 4πr² |
| Cone | Volume = πr²h/3 |

Pythagoras applies only to a right triangle: c²=a²+b², where c is the hypotenuse. For sides 6 and 8, c=√(36+64)=10. Do not use the slant height as the perpendicular height in a cone's volume formula.

**Mensuration examples**

1. A rectangle is 12 cm by 5 cm. Area=60 cm²; perimeter=34 cm.
2. A circular garden has radius 7 m. Using π=22/7, area=(22/7)×49=154 m².
3. A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Volume=π×9×10=90π cm³. The unit is cubic because three dimensions are multiplied.
4. A 10 m by 8 m floor is covered by square tiles of side 0.5 m. Floor area=80 m²; one tile covers 0.25 m²; 80/0.25=320 tiles, ignoring cuts and wastage.

For angles, a straight angle is 180°, angles around a point total 360°, and triangle interior angles total 180°. Similar figures have equal corresponding angles and proportional side lengths. Read diagrams carefully: a drawing is not necessarily to scale unless stated.

## 10. Elementary statistics and probability

For data 2, 4, 4, 6, 9, the mean is 25/5=5; the median is 4; the mode is 4; the range is 9−2=7. The median resists extreme values more than the mean. For a frequency table, multiply each value by its frequency before summing.

Population variance is the mean squared distance from the population mean. For 2,4,4,6,9 with mean 5, squared deviations are 9,1,1,1,16; variance=28/5=5.6 and standard deviation=√5.6≈2.37. Use the formula requested by the question; sample variance uses n−1 instead of n.

For equally likely outcomes, P(E)=favorable outcomes/total outcomes. The complement rule is P(not E)=1−P(E). For two independent events A and B, P(A and B)=P(A)P(B). Example: two fair coin tosses have outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT; probability of at least one head is 3/4. If events are not equally likely or independent, state the model rather than applying these shortcuts.

## 11. Read charts, tables, plots, and maps

Read the title, axis labels, units, legend, scale, and source before calculating. A bar graph compares categories; a line graph emphasizes change across ordered values or time; a pie chart shows parts of a whole; a scatter plot shows paired measurements. A 2D plot has two measured axes; a 3D plot adds a third coordinate, often shown with perspective. Do not confuse visual depth with a larger value.

**Bar chart in table form:** Region A reports 40, 50, 60 units in January, February, March; Region B reports 30, 45, 45. The March difference is 15 units. Region A's January-to-March rise is 20/40=50%; Region B's is 15/30=50%. Equal percentage growth does not mean equal absolute growth.

**Pie chart:** A 72° sector represents 72/360=1/5=20% of the whole. If the total is 250, the sector is 50.

**Map scale:** If 1 cm represents 2 km and two locations are 3.5 cm apart on a straight map line, the straight-line ground distance is 7 km. A road route may be longer. Check the legend, north arrow, and scale before interpreting a map.

For a table, total only the requested rows or columns. For a graph with a truncated vertical axis, calculate the actual difference instead of judging bar height by eye. A change from 95 to 100 is 5 units, not “almost double” even if the plotted bars look far apart.
